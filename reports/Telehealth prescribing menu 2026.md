# Build the Legal Menu, Wall Off ZUMA

As of October 20, 2026, a new cash-pay telehealth brand can lawfully sell a broad menu. Most of it is **FDA-approved drugs**: branded GLP-1s at manufacturer cash prices, ED and hair-loss generics, menopause hormones, tretinoin, rapamycin and other cheap generics. A short list of compounded items also has a defensible 503A basis. **NAD+, topical GHK-Cu and glutathione** sit in FDA's Category 1. **Sermorelin, tesamorelin, bremelanotide and oxytocin** can be compounded because they are parts of approved drugs or have USP monographs. **None of the "research peptides" ZUMA sells has a lawful compounding route today.** That covers BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, semax, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, selank and injectable GHK-Cu. FDA's 503A category lists have not changed since May 14, 2026, despite the July advisory-committee votes. An HHS "interim access policy" that reportedly covers BPC-157 has sat at OMB since October 14 and has not been issued. Controlled drugs (testosterone, phentermine) can be prescribed over video only until the DEA flexibilities expire on December 31, 2026. The cheapest workable setup costs a few hundred dollars a month, not thousands. It pairs a pay-per-visit or API clinician network with an API-ready 503A pharmacy, and routes branded GLP-1s to the manufacturers' pharmacies with no inventory. The most urgent finding is about ZUMA, not the new brand. On August 24, FDA sent warning letters to five research-use-only peptide sellers. Those letters named **retatrutide, tesamorelin and bacteriostatic water**, all ZUMA SKUs, and FDA rejected the RUO disclaimer outright. Lilly sued retatrutide sellers in August. The RUO store is now the bigger legal exposure, and the telehealth brand has to be built so that ZUMA's problems cannot reach it.

A note on numbers before the detail. No compounding pharmacy publishes wholesale prices; Empower, for example, quotes only on request ([Empower](https://www.empowerpharmacy.com/compound-medication/pricing-advantage/)). So every margin on a compounded item below is an estimate from consumer prices, not a sourced fact. Margins on generics rest on cited cash prices. Margins on branded drugs are the membership fee, because the manufacturer sets the drug price.

## Three legal routes decide the whole menu, and FDA hasn't moved since May 14

A 503A pharmacy can compound from a bulk ingredient only in three cases. The ingredient meets a USP monograph, or it is a component of an FDA-approved drug, or it is on the 503A bulks list. Under FDA's interim policy, the agency also does not act against **Category 1** ("under evaluation") substances when the guidance conditions are met. Category 2 and 3 substances, and substances on no list at all, are outside that shield, and FDA says it "would consider taking action" against them ([FDA 503A bulks page](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/bulk-drug-substances-used-compounding-under-section-503a-fdc-act)). As of October 20, FDA's categories list still reads **"Updated May 14, 2026."** Category 1 holds only a handful of peptide or peptide-adjacent items: **GHK-Cu (except injectable), glutathione, NAD/NADH and vasoactive intestinal peptide**. The only peptide still in Category 2 is **kisspeptin-10** ([FDA 503A list PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/94155/download)).

The April 2026 change was real but narrow. FDA removed **12 peptides** from Category 2 after their nominators withdrew them, effective about April 22. The 12 include BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, semax, epitalon, KPV, LL-37, melanotan II and injectable GHK-Cu. Removal **did not** put any of them in Category 1 or on the bulks list. Law firms agree that compounding them still "carries regulatory risk" and is not authorized under 503A ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/FDA-Announces-Removal-of-12-Peptides-from-Category-2-and-Schedules-PCAC-Meetings); [Frier Levitt](https://www.frierlevitt.com/articles/fda-peptides-do-not-compound-list-update-2026/)). On July 23–24 a newly reconstituted Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted to recommend six of seven peptides for the bulks list. **BPC-157, KPV and TB-500 each passed 8–6; MOTS-c 7–5; semax 8–5; epitalon 7–4.** DSIP failed. The committee overrode FDA staff, who had recommended against all seven ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/bulk-list-bound-pcac-backs-majority-of-peptides-in-two-day-public-meeting/); [Orrick, Jul 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/FDA-Peptide-Compounding-Vote-What-to-Watch-at-the-July-PCAC-Meeting)). The votes are non-binding, and listing requires notice-and-comment rulemaking ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/fda-panel-backs-6-peptides-for-compounding)).

Two corrections to claims you will see on vendor sites. First, **sermorelin, gonadorelin and ipamorelin are not Category 1.** FDA's own list contradicts the blogs that say they are. Second, topical GHK-Cu really is Category 1 today, but its status flipped twice in three weeks. FDA removed it on April 22 and restored non-injectable GHK-Cu after a nominator clarified on May 5 that it meant to withdraw only the injectable form ([FDA 503A list PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/94155/download)). One secondary source in the research still describes topical GHK-Cu as removed. FDA's May 14 list overrides that. The flip-flop shows how quickly a Category 1 item can vanish.

Teladoc, LifeMD and Noom have similar arrangements ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/telehealth-firm-ro-partners-eli-143844574.html); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/03/06/teladoc-lifemd-tie-up-with-lillys-pharmacy-partner-to-offer-zepbound.html)). The catch is economics. **No source shows any telehealth company earning a spread on the drug.** The brand earns its membership fee, and Ro's benchmark is $149/month after a $39 first month ([The RX Index](https://therxindex.com/guides/ro-vs-lilly-direct/)). Compounded GLP-1s are no longer a business for a new brand. The Fifth Circuit upheld FDA's tirzepatide shortage decision on **August 27, 2026** ([Fifth Circuit](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/unpub/25/25-10600.0.pdf)). FDA's April 1 policy treats additive combinations such as semaglutide + B12 as "essentially a copy" when strengths are within 10%. It tolerates only **four or fewer prescriptions per product per month** per pharmacy, unless the prescriber documents a genuine patient-specific difference ([Foley](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/04/fda-clarifies-policies-for-pharmacy-compounders-of-glp-1-products/)). FDA's September 2026 warning letter to Empower called its B12 and niacinamide versions "pretextual." It also said that platforms offering prescribers **pre-selected menu options** for the significant-difference statement call those determinations into question ([Buchanan Ingersoll](https://www.bipc.com/fda-warning-letter-to-empower-pharmacy-and-what-it-means-for-503a-compounding-pharmacies); [Lumalex](https://www.lumalexlaw.com/2026/09/24/empower-the-b-vitamin-and-the-word-pretextual/)). FDA sent 30 warning letters to telehealth firms in February and 25 more in June. The June letters punished firms for showing their own brand name on vial images and for calling pharmacies "FDA-approved" ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/fda-takes-aim-at-misleading-online-claims-for-compounded-glp-1-drugs/)).

If ad budget is the binding constraint, swap ranks 3–4 down and lean on the peptide-adjacent items, which ZUMA's audience already wants.

## Wait on two dates: OMB's peptide policy and the DEA's Dec 31 cliff

The "not yet" list exists because two policy decisions are close but unpublished.

**The peptide interim access policy.** On October 14, HHS/FDA sent an "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" to OMB. Its text and covered peptides are not public. AP reported that it would allow compounding of some popular peptides, **including BPC-157** ([Reuters via 93.3 The Drive](https://www.933thedrive.com/2026/10/15/us-fda-takes-step-to-change-peptide-compounding-policy/); [APC](https://a4pc.org/news/peptide-policy-now-under-white-house-review)). Several things could slow it. APC notes a backlog of 50+ HHS actions at OMB. Holland & Knight flags the December 11 funding deadline and expects conditions such as patient disclosures and adverse-event reporting ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/peptides-at-a-crossroads-what-wellness-providers-need-to-know)). PhRMA argues FDA "cannot lawfully authorize compounding via its interim policy while rulemaking is pending," which signals a likely lawsuit ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/FDA-Peptide-Compounding-Vote-What-to-Watch-at-the-July-PCAC-Meeting)). Formal listing through rulemaking is 8–24 months away, depending on the estimate ([PharmExec](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/fda-votes-loosen-restrictions-four-peptides)). Even when access arrives, route limits will matter. **KPV was nominated only as a 0.1% topical cream.** BPC-157's review centered on injection. TB-500, MOTS-c and epitalon were nominated for injection only. So oral or nasal versions may stay uncovered ([FDA BPC-157 briefing](https://www.fda.gov/media/193343/download); [FDA KPV briefing](https://www.fda.gov/media/193346/download)). Hims has said it will offer peptides if regulations permit, so expect a well-funded competitor on day one ([Reuters via 93.3](https://www.933thedrive.com/2026/10/15/us-fda-takes-step-to-change-peptide-compounding-policy/)).

**The DEA telemedicine cliff.** The fourth temporary extension lets DEA-registered prescribers issue Schedule II–V prescriptions by audio-video without an in-person visit **through December 31, 2026** ([Holland & Knight, Jan 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/01/dea-and-hhs-extend-telemedicine-prescribing-flexibilities-through-2026)). The permanent special-registration rule (RIN 1117-AB40) has been at OIRA since about August 25, with a November target. The January 2025 proposal carried an **$888 fee** per registration type. A Federal Register search on October 6 found neither a final rule nor a fifth extension ([WCHSB](https://insights.wchsb.com/2026/10/08/under-90-days-telehealth-prescribing-rules-are-about-to-change-but-the-final-rule-is-still-under-review/); [DeKo Law](https://www.dekolaw.com/post/dea-s-telemedicine-special-registration-rule-just-moved-to-final-rule-stage-target-date-november-2)). Sources conflict on whether the administration intends to finalize at all. Every prior year produced a late extension, but planning around one is a bet.

| Watch item | What it is waiting on | Earliest realistic move |
|---|---|---|
| **BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, semax, epitalon** | OMB interim access policy, then rulemaking | Late 2026 or early 2027, if OMB clears it and no court blocks it |

## A sub-$500/month stack: per-visit clinicians, API pharmacy, manufacturer routing

The networks the founder found too expensive really are expensive. Cuvo charges $9,800 setup plus $997/month plus $25 per consult, and API access requires its $2,500/month tier. Telegra charges $5,000 plus $2,999/month. Rimo starts at $2,500/month, and CareValidate at $2,000–2,500/month. One OpenLoop proposal was $9,000 setup plus $1,500/month on a 12-month term, with OpenLoop holding the merchant account and the brand keeping only 42–59% of patient revenue. Most of these figures come from competitor blogs and should be read as leads, not quotes ([Cuvo](https://cuvo.co/blog/best-telehealth-provider-networks); [Cuvo API test](https://cuvo.co/blog/white-label-telehealth-api-platforms-tested)).

The cheap options are as follows.

For a founder who already owns a storefront, checkout and accounts, the recommended setup has three parts. **First, get a Beluga quote and an MDI quote.** API-first networks with bring-your-own pharmacy let you keep your own front end and be merchant of record, and Beluga takes no revenue share. **Second, use Qualiphy's per-exam pricing as the floor** for the cheapest possible start, accepting its med-spa orientation and 48-state reach. **Third, skip Karpa and FUSE DIY** unless you want to abandon your own stack, because both bundle the pieces you already have and FUSE's percentage fee grows with you.

On pharmacies, split the menu by fulfillment path. **Branded GLP-1s** go by e-prescription to NovoCare Pharmacy or LillyDirect's partners (Gifthealth), with no inventory and no drug margin. **Generics** (ED, hair, HRT, spironolactone, rapamycin, metformin) go to any retail or mail-order pharmacy. Cost Plus pricing of $0.35–0.40/pill shows the cost floor ([MedSwitcher](https://medswitcher.com/blog/cheapest-ed-medication-2026)). **Compounded items** (NAD+, sermorelin, topical GHK-Cu, skin formulas, MIC) need a 503A pharmacy.

Empower is the broadest and the only one in the research with a **public developer API** to "programmatically prescribe, compound, and fulfill," plus sermorelin and NAD+ in its catalog ([Empower API](https://www.empowerpharmacy.com/api/); [Empower categories](https://www.empowerpharmacy.com/compound-medication-categories/)). But it drew FDA warning letters in **April 2025 and September 2026**, and Lilly sued it in July 2025. So use it with a second pharmacy behind it. Belmar, Olympia and AnazaoHealth cover hormones and wellness with no recent FDA action reported, though none publishes an API ([TrueEval](https://trueeval.com/compounding-pharmacy-map)). Two pharmacies are red flags. **ReviveRX** had a Class I recall after tirzepatide-labeled vials turned out to contain testosterone. **Tailor Made**, owned by Noom, pleaded guilty to distributing unapproved new drugs. Any pharmacy offering BPC-157 or TB-500 today is a red flag by definition.

Three costs are not optional. The first is a **friendly-PC/MSO structure with flat, fair-market-value fees**, since revenue-tied fees look like fee-splitting and California, Texas and New York are strict ([Dickinson Wright](https://healthlawblog.dickinson-wright.com/2026/02/telehealths-weight-loss-boom-and-the-corporate-practice-of-medicine/)). The second is **LegitScript certification**, which Google, Meta, Visa and Mastercard rely on for pharmacy and telehealth ads and for MCC 5122/5912 merchant registration ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/pharmacies)). LegitScript publishes no price or timeline, and applications more than doubled in a year ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/telemedicine-applications-to-ad-certifier-legitscript-jump-101-in-a-year/)). The third is a **clinician-driven intake**. FDA's Empower letter attacked pre-selected prescriber options, so ZUMA's pick-a-product checkout cannot be reused as the prescribing flow. The patient can request a category; the clinician has to choose and document the drug. Treat the exact line between "request" and "pre-selection" as a question for counsel.

## ⚠️ What this means for ZUMA's research-use-only store

**This section is the most important part of the report.** The 2026 enforcement pattern is clear. FDA is tolerating the pharmacy pathway while the peptides are under review, and it is hitting research-use-only sellers. On **August 24, 2026**, CDER sent warning letters to five online RUO peptide vendors: Peak Performance Peptides, Royal Peptides, NuScience Peptides, Peptide Partners and TXP Innovations (Tex Peptides). The letters treated their products as unapproved new drugs and **rejected the "research use only" disclaimer** where marketing showed intended human use. One example of such evidence was bacteriostatic water bundled with a "peptide calculator." The products named were **retatrutide, survodutide, mazdutide, tesamorelin, SS-31, PT-141 and bacteriostatic water** ([ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/ruo-ined-five-peptide-vendors-learn-research-use-only-not-legal-strategy); [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/fda-balances-peptide-policy-with-enforcement-against-unlawful-peptide-sellers)). A March 2026 letter likewise relied on website evidence despite RUO disclaimers ([D.J. Holt Law](https://djholtlaw.com/regulatory-alert-the-legal-status-of-bpc-157-in-compounding-and-clinical-practice/)). FDA has sent **14 warning letters to retatrutide sellers since 2024**, and **Lilly filed six lawsuits against retatrutide sellers in August 2026** ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/news/hospitalizations-enforcement-gaps-influencers-tied-to-unapproved-retatrutide-products/); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/peptides-at-a-crossroads-what-wellness-providers-need-to-know)).

ZUMA sells three of the named products: **RT-3 (retatrutide), tesamorelin and BAC water**. It also sells **TZ-2 (tirzepatide)**, the molecule behind Zepbound, which Lilly has already defended in court and in lawsuits against compounders. The rest of the catalog has no lawful drug route. The PCAC-recommended peptides (BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, semax) are still outside 503A. CJC-1295, ipamorelin and selank have no route in sight. Based on the enforcement record, the RUO store is now riskier than anything on the telehealth menu. The prudent move is to **stop selling RT-3, TZ-2, tesamorelin and BAC water now** and to have a regulatory lawyer review the rest of the catalog. Two caveats are inferences, not findings. The research found no source on whether code names like "RT-3" offer any protection, but FDA's willingness to look past disclaimers to marketing evidence suggests they do not. And no source addresses whether peptide blends would be covered by any future interim policy.

| ZUMA SKU | Status today | The lawful equivalent through the telehealth brand |
|---|---|---|
| RT-3 (retatrutide) | Investigational; named in Aug 24 letters; Lilly suits | None. Offer Zepbound, Wegovy or Foundayo |
| TZ-2 (tirzepatide) | Approved only as Lilly's brand; compounded tirzepatide lost in the Fifth Circuit | Zepbound vials via LillyDirect, $299–449 |
| Tesamorelin | Named in Aug 24 letters | Egrifta brand, or compounded with a documented patient-specific difference |
| NAD+ | Category 1 | 503A prescription, available now |
| GHK-Cu | Injectable has no route; topical is Category 1 | Topical compounded cream, available now |
| BPC+TB; GHK-Cu+BPC-157+TB-500 blend | No route; PCAC recommended 8–6 | Wait for the OMB policy; blend coverage unknown |
| MOTS-c, semax | No route; PCAC recommended | Wait for the OMB policy |
| CJC+IPA | No route; 2024 votes 0–13 and 0–12 | Sermorelin as the substitute |
| Selank | No route, no PCAC review | None |
| BAC water | Named in Aug 24 letters as evidence of intended human use | No standalone role |

The telehealth brand must be kept separate from ZUMA for four reasons, all grounded in the sources. **First, intended-use evidence.** FDA's letters hinged on evidence that RUO products were meant for people. A telehealth brand that links to ZUMA, shares customer accounts with it, or treats ZUMA buyers as patients would supply exactly that evidence, against both businesses. **Second, LegitScript, ads and payments.** Marketing BPC-157, TB-500 or RUO peptides would jeopardize LegitScript certification and the Meta and Google ad accounts that depend on it. Card networks use the same certification for high-risk pharmacy merchant codes ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/navigating-legitscript-certification-for-weight-loss-medications-common-inquiries-and-answers)). **Third, litigation.** Lilly sues on Lanham Act and corporate-practice theories, and a shared entity puts the new business inside any RT-3 or TZ-2 suit ([WSGR](https://www.wsgr.com/print/v2/content/49047879/Telehealth-Companies-Sued-for-Selling-Compounded-GLP-1-Drugs-and-Violations-of-Corporate-Practice-of-Medicine-Laws.pdf)). **Fourth, corporate practice of medicine.** The clinical side has to sit in a physician-owned PC that makes its own prescribing decisions, which a peptide retailer cannot steer.

In practice, that means a separate legal entity, brand, domain, merchant account and customer database. Reuse ZUMA's code, not its data, and do not email ZUMA's list to recruit patients without counsel's sign-off. The specific structure is an inference to confirm with a healthcare regulatory lawyer, especially on whether shared ownership itself is a problem; none of the sources addressed that.

## Conclusion

The opportunity has shifted. A year ago the cash-pay playbook was compounded GLP-1s at fat markups. In October 2026 the legal money sits in three places: membership fees on branded drugs, near-pure margin on cheap generics, and a small set of Category 1 and approved-component compounds. The peptides ZUMA's customers want most could become legal by Rx within months. That makes the telehealth brand, not the RUO store, the vehicle that can capture ZUMA's demand lawfully. Build it now on the approved and Category 1 menu, keep it on a non-controlled core through the DEA cliff, and be ready to add BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c and semax the week an interim policy publishes.

The other implication is that legalization does not rescue the RUO model; it ends its reason to exist. Every peptide that gains interim access becomes something a licensed competitor, Hims included, can sell openly with ads and card processing. Meanwhile FDA keeps sending warning letters to RUO sellers and Lilly keeps suing them. The founder's real asset is the storefront, checkout and audience knowledge, not the RUO catalog. The cleanest path is to move that capability into a separately structured, LegitScript-certified brand and wind down the SKUs FDA and Lilly have already named.
